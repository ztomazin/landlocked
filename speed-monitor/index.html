Two things, both from where you are standing, and no tape measure: mark something whose length you know, and trace the direction of the road. The app then works out the road surface's geometry and computes the gate distance itself — the reference does not need to be near the gates.

1. Mark something of known length, lying along the road — a parked car, a lane stripe. Tap each end at ground level. A longer object gives a better result.

How it works, and what it cannot do

The measurement

Speed is distance divided by time. You mark two lines across the road and measure the real distance between them; the software finds moving objects in each video frame, follows them, and records the instant each one crosses each line. Crossing times are interpolated between frames, so the timing is finer than the video's frame rate. No lens calibration or perspective model is needed, and the same reference point is used at both lines so that most systematic error cancels out.

Setting the scale without a tape measure

A single camera cannot know how large anything is — the same image fits a small object nearby or a big one far away. One real-world length must come from somewhere, and that is a fact of geometry, not a missing feature. What it does not have to be is the gate distance.

Mark anything in shot whose length you know and that lies along the road. Vehicles travel parallel to the road, so their paths across the frame reveal the road's vanishing point; that plus the two gates fixes the geometry of the road surface, and the known object then sets the scale for gates placed anywhere else in the frame. No lens data, no camera height, no tilt angle. Speeds measured before the scale settles are filled in afterwards, and refined as more traffic passes.

Accuracy

Against a synthetic scene with a known speed, the software's own error is under 0.5% — press Check accuracy above to run that test yourself. In the field, your error is dominated by how well you measured the distance between the gates. Every measurement is reported with an uncertainty range that accounts for distance error, frame rate, and viewing geometry. To validate a specific setup, have someone drive past at a steady speed shown on their speedometer or a GPS app and compare.

Privacy by design

Video frames are processed in memory and discarded. Nothing is uploaded: there is no server, no account, and no analytics. The tool stores speeds, times, and your own notes — never images, license plates, or faces. It cannot identify a vehicle or a driver, and it is not built to.

What this is not

  • Not enforcement. These numbers cannot ticket anyone and are not admissible as an enforcement measurement.
  • Not a substitute for an official speed study. Its purpose is to show that one is warranted.
  • Not for confronting drivers. Aggregate data brought to a council meeting changes streets; roadside confrontations do not.

Staying safe and lawful

Film from a pavement, driveway, or private property well back from moving traffic — never from the roadway, and never in a way that distracts drivers. Rules on recording in public vary by jurisdiction; check your local ones. Do not film into private homes or yards.

Using the data for policy

The 85th percentile speed is the number that matters: the speed at or below which 85% of drivers travel. Traffic engineers use it to evaluate speed limits and traffic calming requests, so a report built around it is hard to wave away. Measure for at least 30–60 minutes, repeat at different times of day and on different days, and bring the report plus the CSV to whoever runs your city's traffic calming program.
